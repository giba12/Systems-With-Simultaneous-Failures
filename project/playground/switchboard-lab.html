Climb the stairs

The algorithm starts with every switch broken and climbs one floor at a time. It asks "does the ship work?" once per arrangement and keeps only the floor it is on and the one below. Click a square to put it on the board.

When one bump breaks several switches

Slide β or click the chart. At β = 0 switches break alone. As β grows, more breaks arrive as bundles. Two things happen to this ship, and they pull in different directions.

lethal shockbinomial failure rateindependent failures

"Worth of the redundancy" is this ship's life divided by the life of the same switches wired in series, under the same failure model. Bundled breaks tend to make the absolute life longer, because the first bundle takes a while to arrive, but a bundle can take a switch and its backup together. The two bundling recipes agree on a single redundant pair and split apart as more pairs are added.

When do you call the crew?

A repair replaces every broken switch and makes the ship new. It costs a set-up fee, a price per replaced switch, and a fine if the ship had already died. Each rule below is scored by average cost per unit of time.

The health line

Health = a board's expected remaining life divided by a new ship's. Call the crew when health drops below your line. Slide it and watch the cost change. The curve is a staircase because the plan only changes when the line crosses a board's health.

Finding the perfect plan by guessing

Dinkelbach's method. Guess a price for each day of flying, make the best repair-or-fly choice on every board at that price, then measure what that plan really costs per day. Use that as the next guess. Stop when the guess stops improving.

The port covers ships of up to six switches so every board can be shown. The C++ tool goes to 20 switches for the independent model and 16 for the common-cause models. Common-cause models here are the lethal shock and the binomial failure rate, both calibrated so a switch lives one unit on average.
